After this chapter you can operate APM across a whole organization — not one repo, but a fleet. You will wire apm audit --ci into branch protection as a required, protected pull-request check (the integrity gate from Chapter 7 and policy gate from Chapter 9, re-run in CI as defence in depth), stand it up with microsoft/apm-action, route dependency traffic through an approved registry proxy where supported (or prepare an offline bundle handoff), publish and own an org apm-policy.yml baseline, and roll it all out with a measured adoption playbook. This is the capstone: it introduces no new property. Instead it asks whether the four you already know — Portability, Reproducibility, Provenance / security, and above all Governance — hold for every repo, enforced and operated at scale. Governance is the star of the chapter: the org’s policy must reach a protected enforcement point, not merely exist in a repository. This chapter targets apm v0.31.0, compared with the book’s v0.23.1 baseline. Policy enforcement remains early preview and dedicated registries remain experimental. Pin the CLI and integration independently, and distinguish install-time governance from the harness’s runtime controls (0.31.0 policy reference).

From “can this repo install?” to “can every repo install?”

A single team starts with a manifest, a lockfile, and a restore habit: apm.yml, apm.lock.yaml, and apm install. Everything in Chapters 5–9 answered a repo-sized question: does my project install, reproduce, stay current, stay safe, and stay within policy? An enterprise asks a different, larger question — the shift this whole chapter turns on: not “can this repo install?” but “can every repo install safely and predictably, without the platform team in the loop?”

That reframing surfaces five things the single-team story never forces you to build (Making the case):

  • Repeatable rollout — a runbook any team follows, not a hero who hand-holds each repo.
  • Policy ownership — a named, protected owner of the org’s apm-policy.yml.
  • Audit gates — a required CI check on every pull request, authoritative regardless of what a developer does locally.
  • Registry strategy — controlled, mirrored, and (when needed) offline dependency traffic.
  • Exception handling — a visible, reviewable way to grant a waiver.

The docs’ worked figure is a mid-to-large org: 50 repositories, 200 developers, five AI coding tools. Without central management a predictable failure set emerges — manual config drifting per repo, no audit trail (“what agent configuration was active at release 4.2.1?” has no answer), version drift between developers and CI, onboarding friction, and ungoverned dependencies: “the same problem regulated industries spent a decade solving for application code, now back in a new form” (Making the case). These are the Chapter 1 pains, multiplied by the repo count.

Two boundaries keep the rest of the chapter honest. First, consuming policy is not owning policy: a fleet team (including the original pilot) consumes the org policy discovered from its git remote — authorship lives in <org>/.github behind CODEOWNERS and branch protection, exactly the org-remote model from Chapter 1 and Chapter 9. Second, nothing here is a new property: fleet scale is Chapters 6–10 made repeatable, owned, gated, and measured. The common trap is to think “fleet scale is just the single-repo setup, copied N times” — but N copies without central ownership simply reproduce the drift problem at higher cost. The missing pieces are ownership, gates, a registry strategy, and exceptions, none of which a single repo ever needs.

There is a threshold — do not over-adopt

APM is worth this machinery when you use more than three plugins or MCP servers, your team has more than five developers, you need reproducible CI config, you share standards across repos, or you need an audit trail. “Below that threshold, manual setup is fine. APM is designed to help when manual management stops scaling” (Making the case). Name the threshold out loud so a small team doesn’t stand up an org policy repo it doesn’t need yet.

Meridian

Meridian’s platform team spent Chapters 4–10 proving APM on a single pilot, meridian-checkout. Now they scale it to three product groups — Payments, Onboarding, and Merchant Dashboard. The staff engineer’s question is no longer “does checkout install?” but “can all three groups clone, install, and pass CI with the same guarantees, without us hand-holding every repo?” Their answer is four coordinated moves, one per fleet need: (1) make apm audit --ci a required PR check via microsoft/apm-action; (2) route covered Git-hosted dependency downloads through the security org’s approved registry proxy; (3) publish an org apm-policy.yml baseline at enforcement: block, owned in meridian-finance/.github; and (4) ship an adoption checklist so new repos onboard themselves. The fleet gate: apm audit --ci

Run apm audit --ci on every pull request as defence in depth. It checks the manifest/lock relationship, recorded deployment ownership (deployment-ledger-owners), deployed content and consent, plus install-replay drift by default. A resolved policy adds the org’s Governance checks. Do not program against an “eight plus one” total: configuration, policy and early failures affect enumeration. Exit 1 means a blocking failure; exit 0 does not prove that policy was found, that every warning was absent, or that every runtime file was checked (baseline checks).

A cold runner is no longer a reason to suppress drift. With a reviewed manifest, lock and deployed files but no apm_modules or warm cache, 0.31.0 can hydrate lock-pinned sources in scratch without rewriting the checkout. The public-source probe succeeded; making that source unreachable failed closed with exit 1, not a green skip. Cold is therefore not synonymous with offline. Allow the necessary acquisition traffic rather than adding --no-drift to make the job green (recorded cold-run evidence; audit contract).

Authority comes from protecting the enforcement point, not from the letters --ci. Protect the workflow, reviewed manifest/lock and deployed files, selected policy source, credentials, environment, and ruleset bypass permissions. The CLI really accepts --no-policy; an explicit --policy beats that flag, but APM_POLICY_DISABLE=1 still suppresses even explicit policy in the observed 0.31.0 run, including with consumer fail-closed settings. A required GitHub Ruleset check only earns its guarantee when contributors cannot change or bypass those inputs. This is how you operationalize the Chapter 9 authority boundary.

microsoft/apm-action: the turnkey CI path

microsoft/apm-action supplies the CLI and optional project operations; it is not the authority itself. Pin the Action to its reviewed commit and set apm-version: '0.31.0'. The inspected v1.10.0 wrapper otherwise defaults to an older CLI. Its audit-report input runs bare, informational audit, not apm audit --ci; it is not the required gate (pinned Action dispatch).

Choose the job by what you are reviewing. Default mode runs ordinary apm install, which can repair deployed bytes before audit sees them. When committed deployed files are the review contract, setup-only: 'true' installs only the CLI, then an explicit audit examines those files. Cold scratch hydration means this pattern needs no mandatory --no-drift. Frozen installation is a separate, limited structural check (Chapter 5’s boundary), not an equivalent content audit.

The repository token is not authorization to read every private repository in the organization. Configure access to the actual locked sources and policy authority separately. The same CLI gate can run in Azure Pipelines, GitLab CI or Jenkins; the wrapper is GitHub-specific, not the integrity concept (CI/CD integration).

Registry strategy: proxy and air-gapped

A controlled acquisition path makes the lockfile contract operable behind an enterprise firewall. APM’s proxy controls are environment variables, not apm config keys: standardize them in CI secrets and dev containers. Do not infer that APM has only three accepted configuration keys from the defaults its config display happens to list; saved targets and gated registry settings also exist. These transport controls do not cover every APM network request (0.31.0 registry-proxy coverage).

The proxy is a route, not a trust anchor. Review the lockfile and its recorded content hashes, then verify the materialized result. Hashes detect changed bytes relative to trusted metadata; neither a mirror nor a hash authenticates the publisher. The GitHub archive proxy covers supported dependency and marketplace fetches, but not Azure DevOps dependency acquisition, MCP-registry traffic, or policy fetching. Those paths need their own connectivity and authorization.

A dedicated registry is a different package source, not this proxy. APM 0.31.0 ships experimental REST consumption/publication and an implementable HTTP API /v1. That does not establish a Microsoft-operated public searchable hub. OpenAPM’s deferred normative registry work is a standards boundary, not evidence that the client is absent. Meridian can continue using Git and curated marketplaces without adopting a registry server (experimental registries; implemented API).

Org policy at fleet scale

Scale the Chapter 9 ownership model, not copies of unowned policy. Meridian keeps meridian-finance/.github/apm-policy.yml behind CODEOWNERS and branch protection. In 0.31.0, GitHub discovery prefers <org>/.github-private/apm-policy.yml, then the .github, .apm and _apm repositories. GitLab uses the top-level group’s apm-policy project; Azure DevOps uses project apm, repository apm-policy, with legacy _apm/_apm fallback after a 404. Live private-host discovery remains SKIPPED-needs-network: no org repository or authorization was supplied (host-specific discovery).

Select an explicit, reviewed source when the gate must not depend on ambiguous discovery: --policy for CI audit, --policy-source for policy status. Record the canonical host/repository/path and, if pinning policy bytes, the consumer’s policy.hash SHA-256 from the reviewed raw file — not its display name/version or a package’s content_hash. GitHub/registry owner-repository policy matching uses canonical casing; refs, subpaths and MCP identities are not generally case-insensitive. Crucially, an unread remote fetch_failure: block cannot protect its own first fetch: set consumer policy.fetch_failure_default: block. Even a policy-byte hash mismatch only warned and exited 0 in the default-posture audit probe; the consumer block setting made it exit 1 (operations reference OP09.3; policy source and hash configuration).

Local inheritance now works. The tested extends: ./apm-policy.parent.yml retained parent restrictions: allow lists intersect, deny/require lists accumulate, and enforcement and fetch-failure posture tighten. Empty child lists do not erase inherited restrictions. Cold and warm HTTPS-loopback probes retained the same strict fields and TTL; the warm read made no further requests. This is local/loopback evidence, not a live org deployment (tighten-only implementation). If policy freshness itself must gate CI, inspect policy status --check too: the stale-cache probe failed that preflight while audit still evaluated cached rules successfully. Resolution status and rule compliance answer different questions.

Rolling APM out to a fleet is a staged program, not a switch. The official adoption playbook is five phases, each with a single owner, a deliverable, and a gate to clear before advancing (Adoption playbook). The outline’s four-word mnemonic — pilot → measure → standardize → gate — maps directly onto them:

Pitfall: require_pinned_constraint is direct-only

The dependency-pinned-constraint policy check is evaluated against direct manifest dependencies, not every transitive declaration. The 0.31.0 loopback-registry control passed with a bounded direct constraint ^1.0.0 and an unbounded transitive >=1.0.0; making the direct constraint unbounded failed. Evidence: policy-pin-direct-only-control (exit 0) and policy-pin-direct-unbounded-control (exit 1), operations reference OP09.2. A bounded constraint is not an immutable full-graph identity: the committed lock records that graph. An install-time unpinned-transitive warning does not by itself trip this direct-only rule.

Pitfall: audit is not an all-runtime certificate

  • Read the finding, not just the total. Ordinary drift is advisory in bare audit, but invalid deployment owners fail both bare and CI audit. Correctly hashed, Warning-only U+200B content returned bare-audit 2 and CI 0. Critical findings and recorded-hash mismatches fail CI with 1.
  • Native MCP coverage is limited. In the tested MCP-only project, missing native configuration and edited native arguments both returned CI exit 0. Frozen MCP checks compare manifest to lock, not all runtime bytes. Inspect the native configuration too; neither command tests server health or runtime permissions (Chapter 8).
  • Native plugin registration is not audit success. The local-path Agent Plugins 1.0 consumer installed, but CI replay failed with Native Agent Plugin canonical IR is missing, even after the valid metadata control removed a separate consistency complaint. This is a real 0.31.0 limitation, not a network skip or a reason to hide drift. Do not generalize it to every Git-sourced plugin (Chapter 10; core reference §5).

Pitfall: a protected command can still have unprotected inputs

Keep APM_POLICY_DISABLE out of the protected job’s effective environment, including inherited runner configuration. The failing explicit policy became exit 0 when that variable disabled it (policy-explicit-env-only, policy-disable-even-failclosed, operations reference OP09.3). Protect proxy settings and credentials for the same reason. A local CLI result or an unreachable-loopback negative control is not acceptance evidence for Actions, corporate proxy infrastructure, or a live organization policy.

Pin the fleet’s targets, not an expanding default. Meridian keeps targets: [copilot, claude, cursor]. The 0.31.0 default-stable catalog now has nine targets, including grok-build; stable hermes, like agent-skills and antigravity, is explicit-only. grok-cloud remains experimental and outside that default set. Catalog support does not mean every primitive or native plugin works on every target (Chapter 3; 0.31.0 target matrix).

For engineering leaders

Fleet adoption is change management, not a rollout script. The pattern that earns trust is pilot → measure → standardize → gate: prove it on one product group in warn, measure the fleet-wide violations through Code Scanning, standardize the winning setup into an org baseline and a marketplace worth installing from, and only then block the highest-risk rules — narrowly. Two failure modes sink programs: flipping block on day one (every repo with a pre-existing violation fails at once and you spend the day rolling back), and mandating adoption without offering a marketplace worth installing from — carrot before stick. Decide ownership explicitly: “the platform team” is not an owner; a named on-call is. And measure the right things. The report that persuades a VP is measured setup time (the older docs’ 15–60 minutes versus under 30 seconds is an illustration, not a new 0.31.0 benchmark), policy warnings trending down, risky installs blocked, update cadence, and developer satisfaction — not the count of repos with a manifest, which is a vanity metric (Making the case; Adoption playbook).

Recap

  • The question changes at scale. From “can this repo install?” to “can every repo install safely and predictably?” A fleet needs five things one team never forces: repeatable rollout, policy ownership, audit gates, a registry strategy, and exception handling. This is the capstone for Governance — enforced and operated across the org.
  • The fleet gate is apm audit --ci. The same integrity gate (Chapter 7) and policy gate (Chapter 9), operated as defence in depth. Protect the command, policy authority, environment and ruleset permissions; --ci does not neutralize bypasses. Cold scratch replay can fetch locked content and fails when that hydration cannot complete. Pin the Action and CLI independently.
  • Proxy routing is environment-variable driven. HTTPS_PROXY + PROXY_REGISTRY_URL + PROXY_REGISTRY_ONLY=1 route and mirror traffic; reviewed bundles support offline handoffs with one-shot deployment limits. Recorded hashes and ownership support Reproducibility; the proxy is neither a publisher signature nor a dedicated registry. Experimental registry implementation exists; a public hosted hub is not established by this evidence.
  • Adoption is change management. Discover → Pilot → Harden → Scale → Sustain, each with an owner, a deliverable, and a gate. Measure by leading indicators — audit pass rate, drift trend, marketplace uptake, setup time — not by manifest count, which is a vanity metric. Carrot before stick; a named on-call, never “the platform team.”
  • Mind the measured limits at apm v0.31.0. Pin constraints are direct-scoped; consumer fail-closed posture protects first policy resolution, not an environment-disabled gate. A green audit is not complete native MCP/runtime coverage. gh-aw imports isolated context, and the legacy Copilot pack’s shared-skill omission remains a real bug — the successful instruction control is not blanket compatibility.
